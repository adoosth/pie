# The programme

How this project makes progress: the workstreams, who does what, what an
artefact must contain before it may be merged, and how the next task is chosen.
`docs/WORKLIST.md` is the live task list (generated by `pie worklist`);
`docs/REVIEW.md` is the review protocol; `docs/DECISIONS.md` records every
decision that changed the model. This file is the constitution the other three
serve.

## The goal, and the one rule

**Goal.** For any word in any descendent language, say which PIE etymon it comes
from, through which stages, by which laws — and say *no connection* honestly when
there is none. Complete the family's vocabulary hierarchy and etymology by
covering the whole corpus, not by covering the easy part well.

**The rule.** A word is explained only when registered, cited rules turn a
recorded ancestor into the attested word, character for character. Nothing else
counts: not a lookup, not a spelling guess, not "close". A miss is a fact about
the model and is published as one.

**The corollary.** Because the metric is unforgiving, the temptation is always
to bend the *target* (compare case-insensitively, strip a macron, accept a
near-miss) or to bend the *rule* (one rule per word). Both are forbidden. What
is allowed is a new *layer* that is honest about itself and scored separately —
which is exactly how the orthographic layer was added: its own tier, its own
number, never folded into the historical one.

## One pathway per lexeme (2026-11-09)

This is a decision rule, not a preference. It governs proposals,
reviews and what a word page is allowed to claim:

> **Every word in any language has ONE and EXACTLY ONE etymology pathway.**
> Multiple pathways for a word might be proposed, but only one can be ultimately
> true. Multiple words that look and sound the same but have different meanings
> may have different pathways.
>
> This means, for example, that if a pathway is known for a word to be true, and
> another pathway is also sensible for that word, we assume that only one of
> those two things happened that we ended up with that word, and if a pathway
> leads to a word that has another known pathway with a more reasonable and
> similar meaning, then this pathway is probably incorrect.

What follows from it:

- **Uniqueness is per lexeme, not per spelling.** One *word* — one headword in
  one sense — has one pathway. Two homographs (*Arm* "poor" and *Arm* "arm") are
  two words and may each have their own, even though they share a spelling.
  Conversely, a single lexeme never gets two competing ancestors "because both
  fit": a page that shows two full derivations for one sense asserts something
  the rule forbids.
- **A rival pathway is evidence against, not harmless.** A derivation is weaker
  when a known pathway already explains the word *with a closer form and a more
  reasonable sense*. Competing explanations are compared on meaning first, then
  form, and the loser is dropped — not kept as a "possible" alternative.
- **Form alone is never enough.** Similar sound is compatible with false
  friends. When two candidates explain the same shape, the one whose meaning
  history is the more ordinary development wins; if neither meaning is ordinary,
  the word is recorded as unexplained rather than given the cheaper path.
- **The corpus is a set of words, not of strings.** Counts and links that merge
  homographs, or count one spelling once when it is two lexemes, misreport the
  rule and are bugs in the instrumentation, not in the principle.

Consequence for the interface: the word page is addressed by `lang` + spelling,
so a spelling with several lexemes currently renders the first entry and only a
"N entries" badge (see `docs/UI_PLAN.md`, roadmap v1.2). Until the page can separate and list
homographs, a reviewer must not read that page as a claim that the forms belong
to one lexeme.

## Workstreams

Seven streams run in parallel. Each has an owner *role*, a directory it may
write, a definition of done, and a way it is measured. A stream may not write
outside its scope; the integrator merges.

| # | Stream | Writes | Measured by |
|---|--------|--------|-------------|
| W1 | **Sources & lexicons** — find dictionaries, ingest them, repair ancestry links | `data/lexicon/_supplement/`, `tools/ingest_*`, `data/sources.yaml` | glossary entries, `identified` share, ancestry coverage |
| W2 | **Orthography** — how each stage is written | `data/spelling/*.yaml` | `orthographic` count, orthographic rate |
| W3 | **Sound laws** — systematic progress through the literature, one transition at a time | `data/rules/*.yaml` | exact count, per-transition accuracy (`docs/EVALUATION.md`) |
| W4 | **New laws & deliberation** — mine the misses, propose laws, argue them | `proposals/*.md` (never `data/rules/` directly) | accepted proposals, and the exact gain each one caused |
| W5 | **Morphology & paradigms** — which cell of a paradigm a reflex continues | `data/paradigms/`, rules tagged `paradigm` | `exact` on citation forms vs *some cell* |
| W6 | **Review** — the gate every other stream passes through | `docs/DECISIONS.md`, review notes | defects caught before merge; regression rate |
| W7 | **Instrumentation** — coverage, worklist, evaluation, UI, tests | `src/`, `tools/`, `tests/` | reproducibility of every published number |

W1 and W7 are the force multipliers: a language with no ancestry links cannot be
measured at all, and a metric nobody can re-run is not evidence.

## Roles

Subagents act as **linguists**. The roles are deliberately separated so that
the person who wants a rule is not the person who approves it.

- **Descriptive linguist (W1/W3).** Reads the grammars and dictionaries, writes
  rule files and lexicon supplements. Must state the rule in prose, give the
  conditioning environment, and cite a page or a section, not just an author.
- **Historical linguist (W4).** Mines the misses for recurring correspondences,
  proposes laws *as proposals* (`proposals/NNN-<slug>.md`), and predicts the
  corpus gain before anyone implements it.
- **Skeptic (W6).** Tries to break the proposal: does the rule over-apply? Is
  the environment stated too broadly? Is the example cherry-picked? Would the
  rule also fire where it must not? The skeptic's default answer is *no*.
- **Integrator (super agent).** Chooses the next task from the worklist, keeps
  the streams disjoint, runs the gates, publishes the numbers, and keeps the
  documentation true.

Anyone may *read* everything. Only the integrator merges to `main`. An agent may
not both author and approve the same artefact.

## The loop

1. **Triage (5 minutes).** `pie worklist` → pick the largest, cheapest,
   best-evidenced task. The worklist ranks by size; the programme overrides it
   when a task unblocks a whole branch (a missing transition file beats a large
   spelling signature in a language with rules).
2. **Proposal (W4).** Write the claim before the code: the law or convention, the
   environment, three to ten examples from *different* words, the expected gain,
   and the citation. Store it in `proposals/`.
3. **Critique (W6).** The skeptic answers the checklist in `docs/REVIEW.md`. A
   rejected proposal keeps its file and its reason — the record of what was
   considered is part of the model's honesty.
4. **Implement.** One artefact at a time: one rule (or one small, coherent
   rule file), one spelling file, one supplement. No mixed commits.
5. **Measure.** `pie check-rules`, the test suite, `tools/evaluate.py` for the
   transition, `tools/coverage.py` for the corpus. The diff of `docs/COVERAGE.md`
   must be explained in the commit message: what went up, what (if anything)
   went down, and why.
6. **Review (W6).** Re-derive the examples by hand from the trace. Check the
   tier. Check that no target was bent.
7. **Merge & log.** Update `docs/DECISIONS.md`, regenerate `docs/WORKLIST.md`,
   and bump the version when the published numbers move.

A session that ends without a merged artefact is fine if it ended with a written
proposal or a measured negative result. A session that merges an uncited rule is
not.

## Artefact contracts

**A sound law** (`data/rules/<src>__<dst>.yaml`) must carry: `id`, `order`,
`match`/`replace`, an `env` whenever the change is conditioned, `tier`, one
sentence of prose, at least one `references` key from `data/sources.yaml`, and
`examples` whose `in → out` is verified by `pie check-rules`. If the rule is a
hypothesis it is `proposed` or `custom`, never `established`.

**An orthographic rule** (`data/spelling/<lang>.yaml`) is the same shape with
`tier: orthographic` and one extra condition: it must state a *writing
convention*, not a history. Its examples show a complete phonemic string and the
written word. It may never be used to claim a sound change happened.

**A supplementary lexicon** (`data/lexicon/_supplement/`) must record where each
entry came from (dictionary, edition, page) and must not overwrite kaikki data.

**A proposal** (`proposals/NNN-slug.md`) has: claim, environment, examples,
citations, predicted gain, risks (where it would over-apply), and status
(`open`/`accepted`/`rejected`, with the reviewer's reason).

**A decision** (`docs/DECISIONS.md`) is one dated paragraph: what changed, why,
what it cost, and which number moved.

## Tiers, and what each one may claim

| tier | means | may appear in a headline number? |
|---|---|---|
| `established` | standard in the literature, cited, uncontroversial | yes |
| `proposed` | a defensible hypothesis, cited or explicitly attributed | yes, and the report must say which share rests on it |
| `custom` | this project's own idea (e.g. an inherited research rule set) | yes, labelled as this repo's reconstruction |
| `orthographic` | a writing convention, not a sound change | only in the orthographic number, never in the historical one |
| `edited` | a reader's typed form, a hypothesis by definition | never |

A published number must name its tiers (coverage does: "Rules used: …"). A rule
set made only of `established` laws is a different claim from one that leans on
`proposed` ones, and the page must make the difference visible.

## What we measure

Exact-match coverage is necessary but not sufficient — it can be gamed by
narrowing the denominator. The programme therefore reports five numbers
together, and a change that improves one while quietly worsening another is
visible in the same table:

1. **exact** — words the laws reproduce exactly (the historical number);
2. **orthographic** — words the writing conventions then spell correctly,
   reported *separately* and never added to (1);
3. **identified** — words with a recorded ancestor; a drop here means the data
   pipeline lost a link, not that the model got better;
4. **per-transition accuracy** (`docs/EVALUATION.md`) — the rules against
   Wiktionary's own reconstruction pairs, which catches a rule that helps this
   corpus by hurting the language;
5. **worklist shape** — the count of words blamed on each transition, and the
   number of registered rules that never fire (dead rules are false claims).

## The six coverage bands (2026-11-09)

The exact count answers one question and it is not the only one a reader asks.
The model has six bands for every word of every **attested** language; each
stored word belongs to exactly one of them, or to the `ie_loan` facet beside
them, and the coverage page draws them in one bar.

| band | colour | the word |
|---|---|---|
| **fully explained** | dark green | has a PIE etymon recorded in the literature, **the engine carries that etymon down to the attested form** with cited laws that are all recorded in the literature and unchanged, reproduces the attested form exactly, has a reasonable meaning, and the chain itself is recorded. **Every intermediate stage the record names must also be the form the laws produce there** — see below. |
| **fully explained, with adjustments** | light green | either a recorded intermediate stage is not the form the laws produce there, or the word needs a change of ours — to the etymon or to a law — argued in a report and approved by the project. One report for the law and one for the word, in `reports/laws/` and `reports/words/`, both listed in `data/adjusted.yaml`. |
| **partially explained** | yellow | has a recorded hierarchy, but the engine does not carry it from the PIE etymon to the word — it reproduces the word only from a later ancestor, or not at all. This is the worklist: the transitions above that ancestor are missing or wrong. |
| **unexplained** | red | has no recorded etymology at all (e.g. no dictionary entry). With a full glossary this band measures how much of the language is explained rather than how much the dictionary covers. |
| **non-PIE** | dark grey | is recorded as coming from outside the family — Arabic into Persian, Turkic, Semitic, Uralic (`data/non_ie_sources.yaml`). The model is not supposed to derive these. |
| **unidentified** | light grey | is not in our glossary yet. It is an estimate from a language's total vocabulary, never a counted word and never in a denominator. |

One facet sits beside the six: **`ie_loan`**, a borrowing from another IE
branch (French into English, Greek into Latin). It has a recorded origin, so it
is not unexplained, and its origin is inside the family, so it is not non-PIE;
but this chain does not derive it. It is reported separately and is never
green.

Two conditions are easy to miss and both are strict. **Green needs the whole
chain, not a last mile.**  The derivation must start at the PIE etymon and the
laws must carry it all the way down; a word the engine reproduces only from a
later ancestor is yellow, however exactly that last mile is reproduced — Old
Persian 𐎣𐎠𐎶 is exact from Proto-Iranian *káHmah and English *et* from Proto-Germanic
*ēt, and both are the worklist: the transitions above those ancestors are
missing or wrong.  And **green needs the recorded steps**: a word carried from
PIE whose intermediate stages disagree with the record is light green, not dark.

The bands are drawn wherever a word appears, not only on the coverage page: the
word page carries its band beside the headword, and the glossary colours every
headword with it. Those colours come from `data/category_index.json.gz`
(`lang → word → band`), written by the same full run as `data/coverage.json`,
so a list and a number can never come from two different measurements.

### What may move a word into light green

There are two ways in, and only one of them needs a report.

**A recorded step differs (no approval needed).** The project's definition
(2026-11-09) is that *fully explained* means **all** the reconstructed
intermediary steps match what the record says; if even one recorded step
differs, the word is light green even when the start and the end word are
reproduced exactly — **provided the engine also carries the PIE etymon itself
down to the word**; a word it only reproduces from a later ancestor is yellow,
not light green. English *thin* is the worked example: from PIE *ténh₂us the laws
end at θˈin, the English spelling layer writes *thin*, and the word is exact —
but the laws write θˈinuz where the record has *þunnuz*, and so on through the
four stages in between. Such a word is counted as adjusted
because a fact about the record was measured, not because we changed anything,
so it needs no approval. Every such word names its stages (`mismatched_steps` in
`data/coverage.json`, the band line on its page: *recorded step differs:
gem-pro, gmw-pro, ang, enm*).

**One of our changes (approval needed).** A word is never moved into light
green this way by the counter. It needs all of:

1. a report for every law it needs changed — what the literature says, what we
   propose, every word the change touches, and what it costs elsewhere;
2. a report for the word — its record, the derivation, why the change is
   necessary rather than convenient, and its meaning;
3. the report files present in `reports/laws/` and `reports/words/` and the word
   listed in `data/adjusted.yaml`;
4. the project's explicit approval.

Until then the word stays yellow and its reason says *needs approval of our
modification*. A change that is not approved is not counted anywhere as a
success.

### The review backlog

A dictionary entry that contradicts itself (the ancestry names one etymon while
the entry's own cognate set names another), names an etymon the literature does
not have, asserts a chain its prose hedges, or gives a root a sense the word
does not have, is parked in `data/review_backlog.yaml` and listed in
`docs/REVIEW_BACKLOG.md`. Persian باران is the worked example: Wiktionary
derives it from `*weh₁r-` "water" while its cognate set is `ine-pro:wódr̥`, the
family's ordinary word for water. A word in the backlog is read by a human
before it counts; it is never silently repaired and never used as evidence for
a law.  It keeps its band — the rules did reproduce the form — and the band is
published beside a per-language **in review** count (`review` in
`data/coverage.json`), so a green figure always shows how much of it is still on
the reading list.

These bands are defined for **attested** languages. Reconstructed languages and
attested-but-dead languages with incomplete dictionaries need a parallel scheme
(recorded vs. proposed reconstructions; words that existed but were never
written down). That is deliberately later, and the `unidentified` band is the
placeholder that makes the gap visible in the meantime.

## Cadence

- **Every session:** `pie check-rules`, `unittest`, the worklist, one merged
  artefact, one decision entry.
- **Every release:** full `tools/coverage.py`, `tools/evaluate.py --all
  --markdown`, regenerated `docs/WORKLIST.md`, a README progress box, and a
  version bump when a published number moves.
- **Weekly:** triage the W1 backlog (which language has the worst
  source-to-coverage ratio), and re-read the `sterile` rules in the worklist.
- **Before any claim in public:** re-run the number from a clean checkout. A
  number that cannot be reproduced is not a result.

## How a disagreement is settled

Two linguists may both be right about different things. The order of decision:

1. **The record beats the theory.** If a dictionary records a form, no rule may
   claim to derive a different one and call it a match. The conflict becomes a
   `differs` rung and a proposal.
2. **The narrower environment wins.** When two rules both explain the data, the
   one with the smaller, stated environment is preferred and the other is kept
   as `proposed` if it still explains words the first cannot.
3. **The citation decides the tier.** An uncited intuition can be `custom`; it
   cannot be `established`.
4. **The integrator decides, in writing.** If the evidence is balanced, the
   integrator records both positions in `docs/DECISIONS.md` and picks the one
   that is reversible without changing published numbers.

## Non-negotiables

- No lookup table dressed as a rule; no rule whose only purpose is one word.
- No target bending (case, macrons, apostrophes, near-matches) inside a
  historical number.
- No silent data repair: a changed lexicon is regenerated by a tool and the tool
  is committed.
- No published number without the command that produced it.
- No rule without a citation and an example that `check-rules` verifies.
